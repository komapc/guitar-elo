# Objective, measurable tests of guitar (and related instrumental) skill: empirical novice / amateur / expert values

Evidence tags used below: **[LAB]** = peer-reviewed or thesis measurement; **[RECORD]** = adjudicated record; **[ANECDOTE]** = practitioner/forum claim; **[SNIPPET]** = number seen only in a search-engine summary of the source, full text not opened (treat as unverified until checked). Sources were gathered 2026-11-18. Several key PDFs (Springer, Nature, PMC behind reCAPTCHA, core.ac.uk) refused automated fetches, so some values are snippet-level; that is flagged per line.

## 1. Timing accuracy: tapping/strumming variability, tempo maintenance without a metronome, how long tempo is held

### Takeaway
Lab data give usable anchors. Highly trained musicians tap with an inter-tap coefficient of variation of about 5% (percussionists) versus about 12% for less-trained musicians.

When the metronome stops (unpaced continuation, about 27 beats), tempo error has two parts:
- **Group average:** non-musicians rush by about 2–3.5% on average, while musicians stay within about 1.5%.
- **Person-to-person spread:** this is larger. Between-participant SD of the produced interval is roughly 4–6% for non-musicians and 2–4% for musicians.

Only *high-level* rhythmic training reliably reduces variability, and amateurs often look like non-musicians. No study measures "bars held against a gap metronome" directly, so that slider can only be anchored by inference from continuation-tapping data.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB] Baer et al. 2013, Front. Hum. Neurosci. 7:191: groups and task.** Musicians N = 22, "at least 6 years of musical training (M = 13.9 years, SD = 4.3)". Non-musicians N = 17, "minimal musical training (M = 2.2 years, SD = 1.6)". Participants tapped paced (with a metronome) and then unpaced (continuation, no metronome) at prescribed intervals of 400, 550, 700 and 850 ms. — [Baer et al. 2013, PMC3653057](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3653057)
- **[LAB] Baer et al. 2013: unpaced (continuation) tapping, Table 1.** Values are the mean produced interval, with the SD as printed in parentheses:

  | Prescribed interval | Musicians | Non-musicians |
  |---|---|---|
  | 400 ms | 401.79 (10.06) | 390.54 (22.55) |
  | 550 ms | 550.47 (14.23) | 539.68 (22.41) |
  | 700 ms | 689.74 (16.00) | 675.18 (34.88) |
  | 850 ms | 844.97 (34.53) | 827.05 (51.72) |

  - **Table caption and note:** the caption is "Mean inter-response intervals of the Paced and Unpaced phases", with the note "All values in milliseconds. Standard deviations in parentheses." The paper does not say whether the SDs are across participants or within subject.
  - **Protocol:** "Each condition consisted of six trials of 30 cycles … paced by an auditory metronome". The unpaced sequences averaged about 26.7–27.4 cycles for tapping, and the first 2 unpaced cycles were dropped.
  - **How variability was measured:** within-person variability was computed as the CV of unpaced intervals per trial, after linear detrending. Group CVs appear only in Figure 1.
  - **Authors' conclusions:** "non-musicians were significantly more variable in tapping compared to musicians (p < 0.01)". The variability-vs-rate slope in unpaced tapping was 0.00124 for musicians and 0.00236 for non-musicians, a significant difference.

  — [Baer et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3653057)
- **[LAB] Cameron & Grahn 2014, Front. Hum. Neurosci. 8:1003: groups.** Percussionists N = 25: 12.6 mean years of formal musical training, 8.8 years of percussion lessons, 13.5 years playing percussion, about 15.4 h/week of playing. "Nonpercussionists" N = 30: 7.4 years of music training, and only 13 still practising, at about 4.2 h/week. — [Frontiers 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.01003)
- **[LAB] Cameron & Grahn 2014: beat-tapping CV.** In the beat-finding stage, "percussionists had less variable beat tapping (CV = 0.052) compared to nonpercussionists (CV = 0.115)". Over the continuation and switching stages: CV 0.052 vs 0.127 (F(1,50) = 24.46, p < .001). Percussionists also had lower mean absolute asynchrony. Training measures did not correlate with CV within groups. — [Frontiers 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.01003)
- **[LAB] Scheurich, Zamm & Palmer 2018, Front. Psychol. 9:458.** Musicians N = 20 (about 10 years of private instruction); non-musicians N = 20 (about 0.4 years).
  - Spontaneous production rate: musicians 405 ms per beat, non-musicians 306 ms.
  - CV at that spontaneous rate: 0.05 for musicians vs 0.06 for non-musicians.
  - Asynchrony-vs-rate slope: −0.08 for musicians vs −0.19 for non-musicians. Musicians were more flexible across rates.

  — [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00458/full)
- **[LAB] Repp 2010, Human Movement Science 29:200–213.** 31 college students, mostly with little music training, were compared with 9 highly trained music graduate students. Musicians showed smaller asynchronies, lower tapping variability, greater perceptual sensitivity to timing perturbations, and faster correction after tempo changes. — [Europe PMC abstract, PMID 20074825](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20074825)
- **[SNIPPET] Repp & Su 2013 review, Psychon. Bull. Rev.: two points.**
  - Repp (2010) "found no difference in SD asy between musical amateurs and nonmusicians" tapping to 500 ms metronomes. A review concludes that "only a high level of rhythmic expertise reduces the variability of tapping".
  - The same snippet gives a mean SD of asynchrony of about 16 ms (1.6% of IOI) at a 1000 ms IOI and about 10 ms (2%) at 500 ms. Which group these values describe was not visible.

  — [Repp & Su 2013 (paywalled; snippet only)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2)
- **[SNIPPET/LAB] Continuation drift direction.** In synchronization-continuation tasks, intertap intervals drift from the initial tempo, and people tend to drift back toward their own spontaneous motor tempo. They over-produce intervals faster than their spontaneous tempo and under-produce slower ones. Pairs of tappers speed up relative to solo tapping. — [Sci. Rep. 2017 paired tapping (snippet)](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/srep43987); [Scheurich et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00458/full)
- **[LAB] Professional drummers.** Fujii et al. (2011), "Synchronization error of drum kit playing with a metronome at different tempi by professional drummers", *Music Perception* 28(5):491–503, measured hi-hat, snare and bass-drum synchronization error against a metronome across tempi. The error differed by instrument. The full text could not be retrieved, so no ms values are given here. — [core.ac.uk PDF (blocked to fetcher)](https://core.ac.uk/download/228726576.pdf); [researchmap citing paper (snippet)](https://researchmap.jp/yagai/misc/17045637/attachment_file.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Baer Table 1 as percentages (my arithmetic).** All values are unpaced tapping.

  | Prescribed interval | Musicians: mean bias | Musicians: SD | Non-musicians: mean bias | Non-musicians: SD |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 400 ms | +0.4% | 2.5% | −2.4% | 5.6% |
  | 550 ms | +0.1% | 2.6% | −1.9% | 4.1% |
  | 700 ms | −1.5% | 2.3% | −3.5% | 5.0% |
  | 850 ms | −0.6% | 4.1% | −2.7% | 6.1% |

  Mean bias is how far the group's average interval drifted from the target. SD is the parenthesised Table 1 value as a share of the target.
- **What the parentheses probably are.** Paced-phase values as small as 0.93 ms cannot be within-trial tap jitter, so the parentheses are most likely between-participant SDs of each person's mean interval. That reading is my inference, since the paper doesn't say.
- **Bars before a quarter-beat error, under two readings.** A quarter beat is 125 ms at 120 bpm and clearly audible when the click returns. I assume a constant per-person tempo error, which ignores random-walk jitter and so is optimistic.
  - **Reading A, SDs between participants:** a typical person's error is RMS(bias, SD), about 4.5–6.7% for non-musicians and 2.5–4.1% for musicians. A quarter-beat error then builds up in about 4–6 beats (about 1 bar of 4/4) for non-musicians and about 6–10 beats (about 1.5–2.5 bars) for musicians.
  - **Reading B, group mean bias only:** about 7–13 beats (2–3 bars) for non-musicians and 17+ beats (4+ bars) for musicians.
  - **Neither reading is a sourced anchor.** The data cover only about 27 unpaced beats, so anything past about 6–7 bars of 4/4 is outside the measured range.
  - **Calibrate empirically.** It is better to use the site's own gap-metronome results than to hard-code these numbers.
- **Random-walk error makes both estimates shorter.** With CV 0.05 and independent intervals, the SD of cumulative position after n beats is about 0.05·√n beats, which is 0.2 beat after 16 beats.
- **Converting CV to milliseconds at 120 bpm (500 ms beat).** CV 0.05 is about 25 ms of SD and CV 0.12 is about 60 ms. A self-test could map a measured strum or tap CV of ≤5% to "trained/drummer-like" and ≥10% to "beginner".
- **Amateurs may not separate from non-musicians.** Because the snippet says amateurs and non-musicians did not differ at 500 ms IOI, timing-variability stops between "never played" and "casual amateur" may be weakly separated. Bigger gaps appear only at the professional/percussionist level.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures "number of silent bars held" with a gap metronome (a click that drops out for N bars), either in guitarists or in general. The slider anchors above are inferences from continuation tapping.
- No guitar-specific (strumming) timing-variability study by skill level was found. All data are finger tapping or drumming.
- Fujii et al. 2011 ms values, Repp & Doggett 2007 values, and the Repp & Su 2013 table values could not be read in full text. The 16 ms / 10 ms SD figures above are unverified as to group.
- Baer et al.'s parenthesised SDs are not defined in the paper beyond "Standard deviations in parentheses". A writer should not present them as per-tap jitter. Within-person CVs by group are only in Figure 1, so no numbers are available.

## 2. Speed: picking/tremolo rates, maximal tapping rates, chord-change speed

### Takeaway
Hard lab data on guitar picking speed by skill level was not found. The ceiling is anchored by adjudicated records: 620 bpm 16ths, about 40 notes/s, Guinness 2012. The lower and middle stops rest on practitioner claims, such as "<150 bpm 16ths" being a very low maximum. Piano-finger motor data give about 6–8 Hz single-finger maximal repetition. No study measures chord-change rate (C↔G per minute) by skill level. The closest guitar datum is a cue-to-strum time, which is not a transition time: experienced guitarists strum a cued open chord about 0.7–0.84 s after seeing it.

### Cited Findings
- **[RECORD] Guinness 2012 ceiling.** John "Dr Hot Licks" Taylor played "Flight of the Bumblebee" at **620 bpm** (16th notes, which the article converts to "about 40 notes per second"), against a metronome click-track. Adjudication used slowed-down video. The article admits accuracy is "almost impossible to assess" at that speed. His previous record was 600 bpm (2011). — [Guinness World Records news, Jan 2012](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2012/1/dr-hot-licks-sees-in-the-new-year-in-hong-kong-with-new-fastest-guitar-playing-record)
- **[SNIPPET/RECORD] Notes-per-second claims.**
  - Sergiy Putyatov was reported at 27 notes/s (May 2011), and Tiago Della Vega earlier at 21.3 notes/s.
  - Commentators note these "records" use different licks and are not comparable.
  - Taylor himself wrote in 2012 that Guinness no longer judges a "fastest guitar player" category.

  — [Guitar World on Putyatov (snippet)](https://www.guitarworld.com/news/video-sergiy-putyatov-fastest-guitarist-world); [Dr Hot Licks blog 2012 (snippet)](https://drhotlicks.blogspot.com/2012/04/who-is-fastest-guitar-player.html)
- **[ANECDOTE] Troy Grady / Cracking the Code.**
  - This commercial course uses a simple tremolo test across "total beginners to seasoned veterans", including a case study of a complete non-guitarist on day one.
  - It describes a "very low maximum picking speed" as "below 150 beats per minute sixteenth notes", attributed mainly to a "stringhopping" motion rather than a raw speed limit.
  - The exact page for the 150-bpm claim was not individually verified among the troygrady.com hits.

  — [troygrady.com "Test drive your motion" (snippet)](https://troygrady.com/2021/01/16/test-drive-your-motion/)
- **[ANECDOTE] Forum report.** One player managed about 250 (tempo unit unclear) "with a wrist-only motion" tapping on a table, but "can't usually go much faster than 180 when I test on a guitar with a metronome". This illustrates that table-tap rate overstates on-guitar picking speed. — [Cracking the Code forum thread](https://forum.troygrady.com/t/translating-table-tapping-speed-to-tremolo/40402?page=3)
- **[SNIPPET/LAB] Pianists' keystroke rate.** "The maximum rate of piano keystrokes varied from 6.0 to 7.8 Hz among the pianists." — [Sci. Rep. srep15750 (snippet)](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/srep15750)
- **[SNIPPET/LAB] Pianists vs non-pianists by finger.** "No significant difference in the speeds of the index and middle fingers between the pianists and the non-pianists", but the ring and little fingers did differ between groups. — [Front. Psychol. 2022, 861821 (snippet; full-text fetch failed)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.861821/full)
- **[LAB, numbers not retrieved] Finger dexterity study.** A 2019 *Scientific Reports* study compared the maximum rate of repetitive finger movements in 10 pianists vs 10 age-matched non-musicians. The page was paywall-redirected, so no Hz values are available. — [Sci. Rep. 2019, s41598-019-48718-9](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-019-48718-9)
- **[LAB] Chord formation latency, experienced guitarists.** Crump, Logan & Kimbrough 2012, *Music Perception* 30(1):37–47, tested N = 30 experienced guitarists (mean 20.9 years playing, range 5–53). Their job was to name aloud or strum one of five open major chords (C, A, G, E, D) shown as a letter, a chord chart or a photo.
  - Time from display to strum: 699 ms for letter cues, 769 ms for charts, 839 ms for photos.
  - Strum vs vocal naming: overall guitar RT was 769 ms vs vocal 708 ms. For charts and photos, playing the chord took no longer than naming it (762 vs 769 ms; 818 vs 839 ms).
  - Error rates were 5% for guitar responses and 3% for vocal.

  — [Crump et al. 2012 PDF](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf); DOI [10.1525/mp.2012.30.1.37](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2012.30.1.37)

### Inferences
- **Unit conversions** (bpm of 16ths × 4 / 60 = notes/s):
  - 620 bpm 16ths ≈ 41.3 notes/s
  - 27 nps ≈ 405 bpm 16ths
  - 21.3 nps ≈ 320 bpm 16ths
  - "<150 bpm 16ths" ≈ <10 nps
- **Sourced anchors for picked 16ths.** Only two points along the scale have sources: Grady's "<150 bpm = very low maximum" (anecdote) and the record claims (320–620 bpm). Intermediate stops (beginner, competent, shred) have no source. See Gaps.
- **Biomechanical bound.** A single finger repeats at about 6–8 Hz in pianists. Alternate picking produces two notes per oscillation cycle, so a 6–8 Hz wrist/forearm oscillation corresponds to 12–16 nps, about 180–240 bpm in 16ths. This is my physics inference, not a guitar measurement, and it is consistent with forum reports of 180–250.
- **Lab-measured ornament rates on strings.** The only ones found are second-hand, from Moelants 2004 as cited in Rodrigues 2014 (section 6). Across 56 string players and pooled over vibrato, trill and tremolo, the "fast" mean was 12.1 Hz, with plucked instruments lower than bowed. That is about 180 bpm in 16ths, though it is pooled across ornaments and instruments and was not a maximal-speed test.
- **Crump's 0.7–0.84 s is not a chord-change rate.** It is the time from an unpredictable visual cue to a strummed chord, measured from a resting state, so it includes perception and choosing which chord to play. A one-minute C↔G test removes that choice but adds the release of the previous shape. It cannot be converted into changes per minute without data.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed motion-capture or acoustic study of guitar picking speed by skill level (novice/amateur/pro) was found. There is no source for intermediate slider stops: beginner, competent player, shredder.
- No study of chord-transition time or "one-minute changes" norms in beginners was found. The search returned only instructional sites and an MIT robotic-assist NIME paper ([NIME 2018](https://www.nime.org/proceedings/2018/nime2018_paper0090.pdf)) without transition-time data. Teacher-published norms (e.g., JustinGuitar's one-minute changes targets) were not retrieved.
- Single-finger tapping Hz for pianists vs non-musicians (the 2019 Sci. Rep. study, Aoki et al. 2005) could not be read in full.

## 3. Fretboard knowledge and note-naming speed

### Takeaway
No study measures fretboard note-naming reaction time on guitar by expertise. The nearest evidence is two pieces of research.
- **Chords from images:** experienced guitarists name a chord from a chart or photo in about 0.76–0.82 s, with orientation-specific visual expertise.
- **Note names on staff notation:** musical-Stroop studies show note naming becomes automatic after about 2–3 years of practice and stays automatic for years after quitting.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB] Crump et al. 2012, Exp. 1 (N = 30 experienced guitarists): vocal naming RT.**
  - By display: letter 543 ms, chord chart 762 ms, photo 818 ms.
  - RT depended on viewing angle in ways that matched each guitarist's experience. These rotation RTs are pooled across vocal and strum responses. For photos, the player's own view (0°) took 795 ms and the "watching another guitarist" view (90°) took 792 ms, both faster than 180° (895 ms) and 270° (833 ms). For charts, the standard orientations (0° = 743 ms, 270° = 751 ms) were faster than 90° (771 ms) and 180° (795 ms).
  - Sample: 24/30 could read music and 28/30 could read tablature. When asked to demonstrate their skill, "no subject used sheet music".

  — [Crump et al. 2012](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Musical Stroop.** Musicians naming note names written inside note symbols at incongruent staff positions are "significantly slowed down". Note naming "was automatic after only 2 or 3 years of musical practice", and the effect persisted after participants had stopped practising or reading notation for at least 3 years. — [Grégoire et al., LEAD Dijon (PDF)](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/001393-does-a-non-practiced-cognitive-automatism-withstand-the-test-of-time.pdf); [Musical Stroop effect paper (PDF)](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/000957-the-musical-stroop-effect-opening-a-new-avenue-to-research-on-automatisms.pdf)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Notation and pianists' motor speed.** Musical notation measurably affects how fast pianists execute number-to-finger mappings when numbers are superimposed on notes. — [Stewart et al. 2004 (PDF)](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/45/1/PSY-Stewart2004_GRO.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Crump data show that experienced guitarists map a chord image to a chord name in about 750–850 ms, including voice-key latency. This is chord-level, not single-note fretboard naming, and no source gives note-naming times by level. Expert/beginner RT stops for a fretboard quiz would have to come from the site's own data.
- The Stroop findings imply that automatic note-name retrieval is reached within a few years of training. A "fretboard fluency" stop could therefore separate players with fewer than ~2–3 years of deliberate note learning from those with more. This is an extrapolation from staff notation to the fretboard.

### Gaps
- No reaction-time study of naming the note at a given string and fret, by guitar experience, was found.
- The musical-Stroop papers' RT values (ms) and sample sizes were not retrieved, only the qualitative findings.

## 4. Ear: chord-quality identification, major/minor, intervals, absolute pitch

### Takeaway
This is the best-evidenced area.
- **Major/minor, abstract stimuli:** in a large sample (N = 275), about 70% of tested listeners are near chance at telling major from minor tone-scrambles and about 30% are near perfect. Performance correlates only modestly (about 0.5) with musical training.
- **Guitar-specific:** experienced guitarists identified which of five strummed open *major* chords they heard (chord identity, not chord quality) with 8–16% errors, versus 31–35% for non-guitarist musicians, where chance is 80% errors. A chord photo was shown at the same time on every trial. No group-comparison data exist for chord-quality (triad or seventh) identification on guitar.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB] Chubb et al. 2013, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 134(4):3067–3078.**
  - N = 275 listeners across three experiments heard major-vs-minor "tone-scrambles": 32 pure tones of 65 ms each, in random order. The stimuli were not real chords.
  - The distribution was "strikingly bimodal, with one mode very close to chance performance". Roughly 70% were near chance and 30% near perfect.
  - Performance correlated about 0.5 with years of musical training.

  — [Europe PMC, PMID 24116441](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24116441); DOI [10.1121/1.4816546](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4816546)
- **[LAB, secondary] Blechner's triad study.** As reported in the Chubb literature, "many listeners were unable to perform this task with success rates above chance even for pure major vs minor triads". The bimodal pattern was replicated by Dean & Chubb 2017 and Mednicoff et al. 2018. — [Chubb et al. 2013 listing](https://silvaperceptionlab.owlstown.net/publications/51838). Snippet-level: the quote and follow-up citations come from a search summary over several escholarship PDFs from the Chubb lab, and which PDF supports each claim was not matched.
- **[LAB] Crump et al. 2012, Exp. 2: identifying strummed open chords.** Listeners identified which of five strummed open chords (C, A, G, E, D major, recorded on electric guitar) they heard.
  - On every auditory trial a chord photo was shown at the same time, and listeners had to ignore it. "Congruent" means the photo matched the sound and "incongruent" means it showed a different chord.
  - The task is chord *identity* among five major chords, not chord *quality*. Chance error rate is 80%.

  | Group | N | Background | RT (congruent / incongruent) | Error rate (congruent / incongruent) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Experienced guitarists | 30 | Mean 20.9 years playing | 1268 / 1386 ms | 0.08 / 0.16 |
  | Non-guitarist musicians | 30 | Mean 12.4 years playing; 8.3 years formal training; 28/30 read notation; given 40 auditory pre-training trials with feedback | 1847 / 1860 ms | 0.31 / 0.35 |

  — [Crump et al. 2012](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Bidelman lab 2024, Brain Sciences 14(11):1079.** Listeners identified major, minor and detuned triads. Musicians (more than 5 years of training) "were more accurate, though not faster, than nonmusicians". In related work from the same lab, nonmusicians were "only sensitive to major/minor distinctions" and could not detect subtler chord mistuning. — [MDPI Brain Sci. 2024](https://mdpi.com/2076-3425/14/11/1079)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Siegel & Siegel 1977.** Six highly trained musicians identified in-tune intervals with more than 95% accuracy but were poor at discriminating pitches within an interval category. — [Siegel & Siegel 1977 PDF](https://acousticslab.org/RECA293/293Files/293Downloads/SiegelAndSiegel1977_MistunedFifths.pdf)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Absolute pitch prevalence among music students.** The table below gives the rate of absolute pitch (AP) by sample. Some numbers come from secondary summaries of the Deutsch, Henthorn, Marvin & Xu 2006 study (*J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 119:719–722).

The Japan/Poland figures most likely come from Miyazaki et al. 2018 and the Brazil figure from the Frontiers 2016 paper. [Miyazaki et al. 2018 PDF](https://www.human.niigata-u.ac.jp/~psy/miyazaki/Papers/Miyazaki2018.pdf); [Miyazaki 2012 PDF](https://www.human.niigata-u.ac.jp/~psy/miyazaki/Papers/Miyazaki2012.pdf); [Frontiers Neurosci. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00447/pdf); [Deutsch, auditory.org post 2007](https://auditory.org/postings/2007/664.html)

### Inferences
- **Major vs minor alone does not track playing level.** A major-vs-minor question is a poor discriminator at the top of the scale but useful at the bottom. About 70% of tested listeners (N = 275, not a population sample) perform near chance on abstract major/minor tone-scrambles. A player who reliably hears major vs minor is therefore probably above the typical listener. Real guitar chords carry more cues than tone-scrambles, so accuracy on strummed chords may well be higher, though this is untested.
- **Recognising familiar guitar chords by sound seems instrument-specific.** In Crump, guitarists made 8–16% errors against 31–35% for experienced non-guitarist musicians, with a concurrent chord photo on every trial. This suggests recognising familiar guitar chord *voicings* grows with guitar playing, beyond general musicianship. It is a different construct from naming chord quality (maj/min/7), so it supports a chord-by-ear quiz only indirectly.
- **AP shouldn't anchor a guitar skill slider.** It is rare even among Western conservatory students (about 5–18%), so it is not a useful stop.

### Gaps
- No study comparing musicians and non-musicians (or levels of guitarist) on seventh-chord quality identification (maj7 / dom7 / m7 / m7b5 / dim7) was found. Aural-skills textbooks treat it as a later curriculum unit, but no accuracy norms were found.
- No reliable general-population AP prevalence figure was retrieved. The commonly quoted "1 in 10,000" was not verified in this session.
- Interval identification percent-correct by training level, beyond Siegel & Siegel's six experts, was not found.

## 5. Sight-reading by expertise

### Takeaway
Sight-reading research is almost entirely on pianists. Its best-supported level difference is that professionals read about 4 notes ahead versus about 2 for amateurs, with the same roughly 1 s time span. Predictors of skill are fast trills, sight-reading practice accumulated before age 15, processing speed and inner hearing, which together explain about 60% of variance. No guitar-specific sight-reading norms were found, and an experienced-guitarist sample showed tab and memory dominate over notation.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB] Furneaux & Land 1999, Proc. R. Soc. B 266:2435–2440.**
  - Eye-hand span in notes: professionals about 4 notes, amateurs about 2.
  - Eye-hand span in time: about 1 s for both groups, not significantly different; professionals had less variation.
  - Tempo changes the time span: about 0.7 s at fast tempi, about 1.3 s at slow tempi.

  The Europe PMC abstract confirms the qualitative pattern ("professionals looked further ahead in notes … similar time-based spans … tempo had a larger effect"). The exact numbers come from the search summary. — [PMC1690464](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1690464); DOI [10.1098/rspb.1999.0943](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1999.0943)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Kopiez & Lee 2008.** The best combination of predictors for 52 piano students was "trilling speed, sight-reading expertise acquired up to the age of 15, speed of information processing, and inner hearing", explaining 59.6% of variance. A two-class discriminant analysis separating high and low achievers had about 15% cross-validated error. The authors describe a "bottleneck function" of psychomotor and processing speed and a critical window up to age 15. — [Kopiez & Lee 2008 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_61955856); [TU Dortmund repository](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/731f46c7-9397-4e48-b4af-78c608553d99/content)
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Kopiez & Lee 2006, dynamic model.** General pianistic expertise sufficed for easy pieces. At intermediate difficulty, psychomotor skill, processing speed, inner hearing and sight-reading experience mattered more. At the hardest level, psychomotor speed dominated. — [Kopiez & Lee 2006 PDF](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/5556036/mod_resource/content/0/Artigo-KopiezLee-DynamicModelSightReading-2006.pdf)
- **[LAB] Guitarist reading habits (Crump et al. 2012).** In 30 experienced guitarists (mean 20.9 years playing, mean 3.4 years of formal training), 24 could read music and 28 could read tablature. When asked to play for a few minutes, "no subject used sheet music. Some played music from memory but most improvised." — [Crump et al. 2012](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4686/Crump%20et%20al.%20-%202012%20-%20Keeping%20an%20Eye%20on%20Guitar%20Skill%20Visual%20Representat.pdf)

### Inferences
- **A guitar sight-reading self-test could use read-ahead span.** Ask the player to play through a short passage while the notation is hidden or scrolls, then measure how many notes ahead they can still play correctly. About 2 notes would sit at the amateur stop and about 4 at the professional stop, extrapolated from pianists.
- **Sight-reading is a weak skill proxy for many guitarists.** Many experienced guitarists rely on tab, memory and improvisation, so a sight-reading slider should be weighted lightly or offered as optional. This is supported by the Crump sample, though N = 30 from one city.

### Gaps
- No guitar-specific sight-reading performance study by level was found.
- Lehmann & Ericsson (1993/1996) accompanying and sight-reading data, and the actual accuracy and tempo values by level from Kopiez & Lee, were not retrieved.

## 6. Bends and vibrato: rate, extent, pitch accuracy

### Takeaway
There are almost no skill-level data. One small thesis measured classical-guitar vibrato in three players: mean rates of 1.0–5.4 Hz, with the least experienced player slower and less controlled, and a maximum of about 60 cents extent for fret-axis vibrato. For comparison:
- **Singers:** vibrato rates of 5.5–8 Hz.
- **String players:** vibrato extent of about 20–35 cents.
- **String ornaments:** Moelants' 56 players had a mean "slow" rate of 6.4 Hz, pooled across vibrato, trill and tremolo.

No study of guitar string-bend pitch accuracy among players was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB, thesis, N = 3] Rodrigues 2014, "O vibrato no violão: aspectos qualitativos e quantitativos" (UFMG master's thesis).**
  - Players: A was finishing a performance bachelor's, B was a graduate who played mostly popular electric guitar, and C, a fifth-semester student, was "the least experienced".
  - Mean vibrato rates ranged "from 1.04 Hz (lowest mean) to 5.4 Hz (highest mean)". Players A and B had similar rates; player C "presented slower vibratos, also showing less control of speed".
  - Largest longitudinal (along-the-string) vibrato extent was 59.79 cents, played fast at the 11th fret. Extent was low at the 1st fret and lower on steel than nylon strings.
  - Transverse vibrato was less regular, especially for C.
  - The players' mean rates were significantly lower than Moelants' figures.

  — [Rodrigues 2014, UFMG repository PDF](https://repositorio.ufmg.br//bitstreams/dae7dfe1-d51f-47d0-b4f0-36ca5f79b096/download)
- **[LAB, cited within Rodrigues 2014] Moelants 2004.** 56 string players (violin, viol, cello, lute, guitar and others) played vibrato, trill and tremolo at slow, medium and fast speeds. Mean rates across instruments were 6.4, 9.3 and 12.1 Hz respectively. Bowed instruments had "considerably higher" rates than plucked ones (guitar, lute), and low notes were slower. — [Rodrigues 2014](https://repositorio.ufmg.br//bitstreams/dae7dfe1-d51f-47d0-b4f0-36ca5f79b096/download)
- **[LAB, literature summary] Singers and string players.** Singers' vibrato runs at 5.5–8 Hz with 0.6–2 semitones extent. String players' extent is 0.2–0.35 semitone, about 20–35 cents (Seashore 1938; Sundberg 1987; Meyer 1992). — [MMM 2003 paper "Vibrato: questions and answers from musicians and science"](https://www.mcg.uva.nl/mmm-2003/papers/mmm-35/mmm-35.html)
- **[LAB, physics] Grimes 2014, PLoS ONE 9(7):e102088.** The paper models string bending and vibrato physically, with test-string displacements of 7.5 mm and 11.1 mm and bend angles of about 1.31° and 1.94°. It does **not** measure players' bend accuracy or give cent values for real performances. — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0102088)

### Inferences
- **A pitch-tracking self-test could score bends.** Measure the reached pitch against the target in cents (a whole-tone target is 200 cents). No source gives tolerance thresholds by level, so stops would have to be calibrated from the site's own users. The only related figure is string players' expressive vibrato extent of about 20–35 cents, which is an extent, not an accuracy tolerance.
- **Vibrato rate and regularity are measurable skill markers.** In the only skill-graded data (Rodrigues, N = 3), the least experienced player had slower and less controlled vibrato. Rate stability (CV of vibrato period) could serve as an objective sub-score. The evidence is very thin.

### Gaps
- No acoustic study of electric-guitar bend pitch accuracy (overshoot/undershoot in cents) by player level was found.
- No electric-guitar vibrato rate/extent norms (rock/blues bending vibrato) from measured recordings were found. Figures such as "5–7 Hz, 30–60 cents" seen on effect-settings websites are not measurements and were excluded.

## 7. Automated (MIR) assessment of guitar performance: what metrics separate levels

### Takeaway
Automatic guitar-assessment systems overwhelmingly score pitch/chord correctness and onset (timing) accuracy against a reference score. Commercial patents report separate pitch-error and timing-error percentages. No validated study was found that shows which metric thresholds separate novice from advanced guitarists.

### Cited Findings
- **[LAB, snippet-level] Review statements.**
  - "Most systems that provide assessment of a performed musical piece are based on pitch and onset accuracy assessment".
  - "Most of the computational systems for music education rely only on pitch and timing accuracy assessment".
  - The UPF bachelor's thesis introduces a public "Five Guitar" dataset of 90 recordings built on chroma and pitch features. It warns that room acoustics and recording setup can bias model performance.

  — [UPF thesis "Automatic guitar performance assessment: datasets, algorithms and metrics"](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/4cd08707-b8e2-415e-ab05-c3e4c16476aa)
- **[LAB, abstract-level] Eremenko et al. 2020 (CSEDU).** The paper presents a complete assessment system for guitar exercises (the Music Critic / UPF line of work) and discusses challenges for professional learning contexts. The full text was access-denied, so no metric values are available. — [Eremenko et al. 2020, DOI 10.5220/0009817006290640](https://unpaywall.org/10.5220%2F0009817006290640)
- **[LAB, work in progress] ISMIR 2025 late-breaking demo.** Score-informed guitar transcription is aligned to a reference score, and the system flags pitch, rhythm, tempo, structural and intonation errors. — [ISMIR 2025 LBD 482](https://ismir2025program.ismir.net/lbd_482.html)
- **[Patent] Scoring by error type.** Deviations in pitch or timing are scored as errors, and "a percentage of pitch errors and a percentage of timing errors can also be determined separately". This is a commercial design, not validated against skill levels. — [US8785757B2 "Musical instruction and assessment systems"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8785757); [US8957296 "Chord training and assessment systems"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8957296)

### Inferences
- **The quiz's on-page self-tests line up with what MIR systems score.** The one-minute timer, the gap metronome (timing) and the chord-by-ear quiz map onto the same two dimensions: pitch/chord correctness and onset timing. A natural extension is to report timing error as the SD of onsets against the click in ms (or % of the beat), as the tapping literature does. That makes the self-tests comparable with the CV ≈ 0.05 (trained) vs ≈ 0.12 (less trained) anchors in section 1.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that validates specific timing-error (ms) or pitch-error (%) thresholds as boundaries between guitar skill levels.
- No published research from Yousician or Rocksmith with level-wise performance statistics was found in this session. Lerch et al.'s 2020 review of music performance analysis (TISMIR) and the Florida Bandmasters-based assessment studies (Vidwans et al. 2017; Pati et al. 2018) were not retrieved; they are non-guitar but relevant for method.
