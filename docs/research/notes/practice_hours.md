# Practice hours vs. achievement level in musicians (with focus on guitarists)

Labels used below:
- **VERIFIED** — I read the number in the full text of the paper or document (PDF downloaded and text-extracted).
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY** — read in full text, but in a peer-reviewed paper that reports someone else's result.
- **SNIPPET-ONLY** — seen only in a search-engine summary or a popular/blog source, not checked against the original. Do not hard-code without checking.
- **NORMATIVE** — a figure from a regulator or curriculum: a planned or estimated workload, not measured practice.

Research date: 2026-11-18.

## Q1. Sloboda, Davidson, Howe & Moore 1996: cumulative practice hours to each UK grade (plus Sloboda & Howe 1991 and related work)

### Takeaway
One number from Sloboda et al. (1996) is solid: about **3,300 hours** of formal practice to reach **Grade 8**. Two independent peer-reviewed texts confirm that this held "irrespective of the ability group", so high achievers did not need fewer hours per grade; they piled them up faster. Individual spread is wide: some students passed grades on very little practice, and others did about 4× the average for a given grade. I could not retrieve the per-grade table (Grades 1–8 by group, with SD or range). The article is paywalled.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation and sample.** Sloboda, J. A., Davidson, J. W., Howe, M. J. A. & Moore, D. G. (1996). The role of practice in the development of performing musicians. *British Journal of Psychology* 87, 287–309. — [Wiley DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1996.tb02591.x); [Univ. Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1346170-the-role-of-practice-in-the-development-of-performing-musicians)
  - SNIPPET-ONLY: 257 young people aged 8–18. This comes from abstract and search summaries; I did not read it in full text.
  - VERIFIED-SECONDARY (Barry & Hallam 2002, below): 5 groups of differing achievement, including children who gave up.
  - Semantic Scholar marks the paper `CLOSED`; the academia.edu copy is behind Cloudflare.
- **What "practice" means in the 3,300 h figure.** Neither verified quote says "formal". Howe et al. 1998 say "practice", and Williamon & Valentine say "deliberate practice", glossed as "formal, task-oriented" practice. Read the 3,300 h as instrument practice reported by the child and checked with parents. It excludes ensemble rehearsal and performing, and probably most informal playing.
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (co-authors' own summary).** "Sloboda et al. (1996) supplemented retrospective data on practice with concurrent diary-based information. They confirmed the strong positive correlation between practice and achievement, which was largest for the more formal and deliberate kinds of practice activities, such as scales and exercises. Achieving the highest level (Grade 8) of the British Associate Board examinations in performing music required an average of approximately 3,300 hours of practice irrespective of the ability group to which the young people in the study were assigned."
  - Same passage: correlations between performance and practice in music students "range from approximately +0.3 to above +0.6 (Lehmann 1995)".
  - Source: Howe, Davidson & Sloboda (1998), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 21, p. 405 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X9800123X); [Cambridge PDF](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X98271238/resource/name/S0140525X9800123Xa.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (independent confirmation).** "Regardless of group classification, Sloboda et al. found that all musicians who reached Grade 8 (on Associated Board and Guildhall School of Music Examinations Grades) had devoted approximately 3300 hours to deliberate practice. Moreover, those who had given up musical performance had fallen behind in their accumulation of deliberate practice as early as age 8."
  - The practice estimates were cross-checked with parents.
  - Source: Williamon & Valentine (2000), *British Journal of Psychology* 91, 353–376, p. 357 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712600161871); [PDF mirror](https://www.cglib.org/wp-content/uploads/cglib.org/Musicology/Quantity%20and%20Quality%20of%20Musical%20Practice.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (spread).** Sloboda et al. (1996) "compared five groups of young musicians of differing capabilities and found greater levels of practice at all ages from the 'best' group. This increased over time leading to large cumulative differences, although there was wide individual variation. Some individuals in all groups managed to attain grade examination passes on very little practice. There were others who did four times as much practice as the average to attain a given grade. The greatest extremes were in the highest achieving group."
  - Source: Barry & Hallam (2002), "Practicing", in Parncutt & McPherson (eds.), *The Science and Psychology of Music Performance*, p. 3 of the UCL preprint — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1507397/1/Barry2002Practicing151.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (criticism of the grade measure).** Ellen Winner's BBS commentary: "Sloboda et al.'s own (1996) study … yielded no individual differences in the amount of practice needed to make successive grades. Furthermore, the measures of achievement were not fine grained: passing minimally and passing with distinction were treated as equivalent." — [Howe et al. 1998 with commentaries, Cambridge PDF](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X98271238/resource/name/S0140525X9800123Xa.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (companion diary study, Sloboda & Davidson 1996).** "over the entire learning period high achievers amass twice as much formal practice as moderate achievers, four times as much as underachievers and up to eight times as much as students who give up lessons. At the same time high achievers also participate in more informal practice." — Zhukov (2009), *Australian Journal of Music Education* 2009(1), p. 4 — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ912405.pdf)
- **SNIPPET-ONLY (popular sources).**
  - About 200 h to get from Grade 1 to Grade 2, "the same for every group"; about 800 h from Grade 6 to Grade 7 — [Medium, "Are You Musically Talented?"](https://medium.com/@littlebrown/are-you-musically-talented-f1cf675a49a7)
  - About 450 h to reach Grade 1 and 3,300 h to reach Grade 8 — [teacher blog, R. Inwood](https://www.rachaelinwoodmusic.co.uk/post/why-practice)
  - Neither has been checked against the paper's tables.
- **Context: other classical cumulative figures.** VERIFIED-SECONDARY from Barry & Hallam 2002, citing Ericsson et al. 1993 and Sosniak 1985 — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1507397/1/Barry2002Practicing151.pdf)
  - Ericsson et al. 1993, hours accumulated by age 18:

    | Violinist group | Hours by age 18 |
    |---|---|
    | International standing | about 7,410 |
    | "Good" | 5,310 |
    | Music-teacher track | 3,420 |

  - Sosniak 1985: reaching international standing typically takes about 16 years of practice, at about 25 h/week by adolescence, rising to as much as 50 h/week.
  - Howe et al. 1998 (p. 405) gives the best Ericsson students "approximately 10,000 hours" by age 21 — [Cambridge PDF](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X98271238/resource/name/S0140525X9800123Xa.pdf)
- **Weekly practice by current grade.** VERIFIED; cross-sectional, not cumulative.
  - Source: Hallam, Varvarigou, Creech & Papageorgi, "Are there differences in practice depending on the instrument played?", Table 2 — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10094194/3/Hallam_POM%20Practice%20time%20and%20strategies%20by%20instrument%20group_IP_MV%20ARC.pdf)
  - Sample: 3,325 UK children from beginner to Grade 8. Average weekly practice in minutes, all instruments, mean (SD):

    | Grade 1 | 116 (97) |

    | Grade 3 | 140 (121) |

    | Grade 6 | 232 (234) |
    | Grade 7 | 314 (281) |

  - Practice by instrument group, most to least: keyboard > strings > brass > woodwind.
  - No guitar group is reported. The analysis covers classical orchestral instruments and keyboard.

### Inferences
- **Calibration anchor.** "About 3,300 h ⇒ Grade 8" is the best-verified music anchor and sits between chess club level and Gobet & Campitelli's 2200-Elo mean of about 11,000 h.
- **Plausible range around that anchor.** The text gives "4× the average" for the high end and passes on "very little" practice for the low end. That brackets Grade 8 at roughly <1,000 h to about 13,000 h. This bracket is my extrapolation; no source reports these endpoints.
- **Shape of the curve.** If the snippet figures are right (about 450 h to Grade 1, about 200 h for Grade 1→2, about 800 h for Grade 6→7, about 3,300 h to Grade 8), each later grade costs more hours. That convex hours-per-level shape is what a log-hours model predicts.
- **SD is not reconstructable from the weekly table.** The Hallam weekly-practice SDs are about equal to the means at every grade (CV ≈ 0.8–1.6). Integrating weekly practice across grades would need longitudinal data, which this source does not have.

### Gaps
- **The per-grade table itself.** Exact cumulative hours per grade by group from Sloboda et al. 1996, with SD or min/max, was not retrieved. Library access to BJP vol. 87 is needed.
- **The "<20% of the mean" claim.** A search summary said some individuals at each grade did "less than 20 per cent of the mean amount of practice for that grade". I could not identify the page it came from, so it is unverified.
- **Sloboda & Howe 1991.** *Psychology of Music* 19, 3–21: biographical interviews with 42 pupils of a specialist music school. No hour figures were retrieved.
- **Hallam 2013.** "What predicts level of expertise attained…" (*Psychology of Music* 41(3), 265–289) is cited in the Hallam papers. Its regression figures (variance explained by cumulative practice) were not retrieved.

## Q2. Platz, Kopiez, Lehmann & Wolf 2014 meta-analysis

### Takeaway
The meta-analysis pools 13 studies (N = 788). The correlation between task-specific accumulated practice and objectively measured musical achievement is **r_c = .61** after correction for attenuation, 95% CI [.54, .67]. The observed weighted mean is **r = .54**. Almost every sample is pianists, mostly on sight-reading or scale-evenness tasks. There are no guitarists.

### Cited Findings
All VERIFIED from the full text: Platz, F., Kopiez, R., Lehmann, A. C. & Wolf, A. (2014). The influence of deliberate practice on musical achievement: a meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:646 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00646)
- **Inclusion criteria.** Studies had to report durations of task-specific accumulated practice as the predictor and an objectively assessed musical achievement as the outcome. Searches covered 1992–2012. The final set was k = 13, N = 788 (abstract; Table 2).
- **Result.** r_c = 0.61 [0.54, 0.67]; weighted mean observed r_o = 0.54; true-score variance S²ρ = 0.
  - 81.2% of the variance in corrected correlations is attributable to artifacts.
  - The studies are homogeneous: Q(12) = 8.19, p = .77, I² = 0.00 (Table 4 note).
- **Included studies with N and observed r** (Tables 3 and 4):

  | Study | Sample | N | Observed r |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Kornicke 1992 | College pianists, sight-reading | 73 | .50 |
  | Ericsson et al. 1993, Study II | Pianists | 24 | \|.86\| |
  | Lehmann & Ericsson 1996 | — | 16 | .72 |
  | Krampe & Ericsson 1996, Study I | Pianists, beginner to professional | 48 | \|.62\| |
  | Hallam 1998 | Beginners; accumulated practice vs ABRSM grade | 109 | .67 (corrected .74) |
  | Meinz 2000 | Pianists | 107 | .57 |
  | Tuffiash 2002 | — | 135 | .43 |
  | McPherson 2005 | Beginners; 3-year accumulated practice vs expert rating | 99 | .57 |
  | Jabusch et al. 2007 | Child pianists | 30 | \|.46\| |
  | Kopiez & Lee 2008 | Pianists; sight-reading practice up to age 18 | 52 | .36 |
  | Jabusch et al. 2009 | — | 19 | .44 |
  | Meinz & Hambrick 2010 | Pianists | 57 | .56 |
  | Kopiez et al. 2012, Study II | — | 19 | .42 |

- **Hambrick et al. 2014's music subset, re-aggregated** (Platz Table 1). Eight studies (Lehmann & Ericsson 1996; Meinz 2000; Tuffiash 2002; Kopiez & Lee 2008; Ruthsatz et al. 2008 studies 1, 2A and 2B; Meinz & Hambrick 2010) give a fixed- and random-effects mean of **r = 0.44**.
  - The CIs printed in that table carry minus signs on almost every lower bound, for example ".58 (−.46, .68)" at N = 135. That looks like a typesetting error; use r and N only.
- **Platz et al. on the r² reading.** They object to reading r as r² ("practice explains about 30% of variance", the Hambrick et al. 2014 phrasing). They translate r_c = .61 into Cohen's d = 1.52.
- **Hambrick's own corrected estimate.** VERIFIED in the Platz text (Discussion). Hambrick et al. 2014 obtained r_c = 0.52, 95% CI [0.43, 0.64], against Platz's 0.61 [0.54, 0.67]. Hambrick's "deliberate practice explained about 30% of the reliable variance in music performance" corresponds to this .52.
- **Platz on Hambrick's search.** VERIFIED (section "Criteria-related literature selection"): "While Hambrick et al. (2014) performed a more intuitive search, resulting in a significant heterogeneity of the study sample, the aim of our method was to arrive at a homogenous sample".
- **Hambrick et al.'s reply.** SNIPPET-ONLY, from a search summary; I did not read the full text. The .61 leaves room for other predictors: two additional orthogonal predictors of about r = .56 each would fit. They dispute Platz's characterization of their search as "intuitive" and Platz's objection to the r² reading. — [Front. Psychol. 5:751](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00751)

### Inferences
- **Converting to variance explained** (my arithmetic, which Platz et al. reject as an interpretation):
  - r_c = .61 gives r² ≈ .37.
  - r_o = .54 gives ≈ .29.
  - Hambrick's corrected .52 gives ≈ .27 (their "about 30%").
  - The uncorrected .44 from Platz's re-aggregation gives ≈ .19. It is not comparable with the corrected figures above.
  - The guitar-elo calibration currently uses about 1/3 of variance explained (chess). On corrected correlations the band is about .27–.37, so a similar noise level for music is defensible.
- **The .61 likely overstates "hours vs general playing level".** It applies to narrow, task-matched pairs: sight-reading practice predicting sight-reading, or scale practice predicting scale evenness. Hambrick's set is also mostly piano sight-reading, so no estimate in hand measures "total hours vs overall playing level" cleanly. Hallam 1998 (accumulated practice vs ABRSM grade, r = .67, N = 109 beginners) is the closest.

### Gaps
- No guitar sample was included.
- With k = 13 and homogeneous studies, no moderator analysis was possible by instrument, genre or age.

## Q3. Guitarist-specific studies (classical, jazz, rock and popular, self-taught)

### Takeaway
**I found no peer-reviewed study that reports cumulative practice hours for guitarists by skill level.**
- **Survey data that does separate popular from classical** comes from Menze & Gembris (2018), an online survey of 540 German musicians. It does not break out guitar specifically.
  - Professional popular musicians report **5.3 h/week** of practice against **17.7 h/week** for classical professionals.
  - Hobbyists practise about **3.5–4 h/week** in both genres.
- **Guitar-specific hour numbers** come only from:
  - Fender/YouGov consumer data;
  - exam-board regulatory estimates (Trinity Rock & Pop guitar);
  - the Russian children's music-school curriculum.

  None of these measures accumulated practice against achieved level.

### Cited Findings
- **Menze & Gembris (2018), popular vs classical.** "Played it till my fingers bled: Domain-related fields of practicing and gaining expertise in popular music", Paderborn University, conference poster (not peer-reviewed journal data). VERIFIED from the poster — [poster PDF](https://kw.uni-paderborn.de/fileadmin-kw/fakultaet/Institute/ibfm/Downloads/Poster_Played_it_till_my_fingers_bled_JM-HG_2018.pdf)
  - **Sample.** 540 musicians via online survey; 60% popular, 17% classical, 22% both. Music was a hobby for 67%, a secondary occupation for 19% and the profession for 14%. Mean age 30.4 (SD 12.7, range 12–76).
  - **Practice in h/week (Tab. 2), mean (SD):**

    | Group | Classical | Popular | Test |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Hobby | 3.51 (4.11), n = 59 | 4.08 (4.31), n = 213 | n.s.: F(1,270) = .82, p = .37 |
    | Profession | 17.68 (16.56), n = 19 | 5.33 (8.38), n = 35 | F(1,52) = 13.35, p = .001, η² = .20 |

    The authors' summary: professional classical musicians "spend about three times as many hours practicing" as popular professionals.
  - **Starting age, lessons and self-assessment** (Tab. 1): see Q6.
  - **How they practise.** Popular musicians work on technique and scales less often, and rehearse their own songs more often (Fig. 2).
- **Fender / YouGov, US guitar beginners** (consumer survey, not academic). VERIFIED from the press release — [PR Newswire, Fender 2021](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/16-million-americans-learned-to-play-guitar-in-the-last-two-years-why-and-who-are-they-301394808.html)
  - About 10,000 respondents (methodology says 10,644), YouGov online panel, fieldwork 8 May – 13 June 2021, weighted to US ages 13–64.
  - 7% of US residents aged 13–64 (about 16 million) started guitar in the prior two years. 72% of new players are aged 13–34.
  - **53% of beginners practise ≤2 hours per week.**
  - On average, beginners *believe* it takes **4 h/week for 1–2 years** to "get good". That is roughly 200–400 h, and it is a belief, not a measurement.
  - "One in four have stagnated in their progress."
- **Degner, Lehmann & Gruber (2003), jazz guitar.** "Expert learning in the domain of jazz guitar music", 5th ESCOM conference proceedings. Only the bibliographic record was found; no hour figures were retrieved. — [Univ. Regensburg record](https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/26184/)
- **Längler, Brouwer, Timmermans & Gruber (2021), popular guitarists.** *Psychology of Music*, "Exploring change in networks supporting the deliberate practice of popular musicians". This is interview and network research (participants mostly rock/pop/jazz guitarists and bassists). It cites Längler, Nivala & Gruber (2018, *Musicae Scientiae*) on popular-music guitarists' support networks. I found no hour totals in what I read. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356211003961)
- **Lucy Green (2002), informal learning.** *How Popular Musicians Learn* is a qualitative study of 14 popular musicians aged 15–50 in "Anglo-American guitar-based pop and rock music". The central method is copying recordings by ear, self-directed and with peers. It gives no hour quantification. (SNIPPET-ONLY from search summaries) — [UCL IOE project page](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/research-projects/2018/oct/adapting-popular-musicians-practices-classroom); [Wikipedia: Lucy Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Green)

### Inferences
- **Practice is a small share of a popular guitarist's playing time.** Popular professionals log only about 5 h/week of "practice". Most of their instrument time is presumably rehearsal, gigging, jamming and songwriting, which surveys do not count as practice.
  - A guitar quiz that maps to "practice hours" will therefore *understate* the instrument time of pop/rock players relative to classical players at a comparable level.
  - Consider defining the quiz's unit as "hours of playing (practice + rehearsal + gigs)", or apply a genre correction.
- **Dose for a typical pop hobbyist.** At about 4 h/week, a decade of hobby playing is about 2,100 h of practice (4.08 × 52 × 10). That is my arithmetic and assumes constant weekly practice.

### Gaps
- **Measured guitar data.** No peer-reviewed dataset gives cumulative hours against skill level for classical, jazz, rock or self-taught guitarists.
- **Degner et al. 2003.** Its jazz-guitar data, possibly retrospective practice estimates, would need the ESCOM 2003 proceedings.
- **Kaczmarek (2012).** A German study of gifted popular-music youth, cited in the Menze & Gembris poster. Not retrieved.
- **Practice diaries.** No diary study of guitar students was found.

## Q4. Popular and jazz musicians: informal practice vs deliberate practice in predicting skill

### Takeaway
In classical samples, **formal or deliberate practice** (scales, exercises, task-specific work) predicts grade attainment best. Top achievers also do more *informal* playing. No quantitative jazz or popular study comparing the two kinds of practice as predictors of skill was found. The evidence for popular music is descriptive: less formal practice, more self-directed song learning.

### Cited Findings
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY.** In Sloboda et al. 1996, the practice–achievement correlation "was largest for the more formal and deliberate kinds of practice activities, such as scales and exercises" — [Howe et al. 1998, p. 405](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X98271238/resource/name/S0140525X9800123Xa.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY.** High achievers do 2× the formal practice of moderate achievers and 4× that of underachievers, and "also participate in more informal practice" (Sloboda & Davidson 1996, via Zhukov 2009) — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ912405.pdf)
- **VERIFIED.** Hours alone do not predict quality on a given piece. In 22 pianists across 4 skill levels learning one Bach piece, "Quantity of practice, however, was not significantly related to quality of performance"; how practice was segmented mattered more — [Williamon & Valentine 2000](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712600161871)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY.** Barry & Hallam 2002: cumulative practice "may be a good predictor of the overall level of expertise attained it may not predict the quality of performance at any point in time" (citing Hallam 1998a; Williamon & Valentine 2000) — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1507397/1/Barry2002Practicing151.pdf)
- **VERIFIED (genre differences in what practice consists of).** Menze & Gembris 2018: popular musicians practise technique and scales less often and rehearse their own songs more often. 33% of popular musicians use video tutorials (very) frequently vs 10% of classical; 44% use scores or tabs vs 84% — [poster PDF](https://kw.uni-paderborn.de/fileadmin-kw/fakultaet/Institute/ibfm/Downloads/Poster_Played_it_till_my_fingers_bled_JM-HG_2018.pdf)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY (popular-music performance hours as practice).** Weisberg's BBS commentary: by 1961 Lennon and McCartney "had put in some 1,200 hours in performance alone… This averages close to one performance per day. This figure was surely augmented by practice sessions," and the early repertoire was mostly faithful covers.
  - This is an estimate in a commentary, not measured data.
  - It is unrelated to Gladwell's popular "10,000 hours / Hamburg" framing.
  - Source: [Howe et al. 1998 with commentaries](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X98271238/resource/name/S0140525X9800123Xa.pdf)
- **SNIPPET-ONLY (jazz).** A case study of a jazz pianist memorizing a piece compared preparation time with classical figures of about 10 h of practice per minute of performance (Chaffin & Imreh 2002) and 15 h per minute (Lehmann & Ericsson 1998). This is about preparing one piece, not career hours — [UConn Music Lab PDF](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Informal playing should be discounted, not ignored.** For a guitar quiz, an hour of informal playing (jamming, strumming known songs) is probably worth less skill per hour than formal practice, but it is not zero. Sloboda's high achievers did more of both.
- **No study gives the exchange rate.** I found no source that quantifies a conversion such as "1 h informal = x h formal".

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of jazz or popular musicians regresses skill on formal vs informal practice hours. Search attempts returned only blogs and anecdotes, for example Charlie Parker practising "up to 15 hours a day" (unverified).

## Q5. Twin and genetic studies: how much variance practice explains

### Takeaway
- **Mosing et al. 2014.** In 10,539 Swedish twins, lifetime practice correlates only **r = .18–.36** with *auditory discrimination* ability.
  - Practice itself is **41% (women) to 69% (men) heritable**.
  - Within identical-twin pairs, the twin who practised more did not score higher, even with pair differences up to **20,228 h**.
  - Caveat: the outcome is a perception test in the general population, not playing level, so it should not be set directly against Platz's r = .61.
- **Hambrick & Tucker-Drob.** The outcome is a *self-reported* accomplishment (yes/no) and practice is a 3-category self-report. Their polychoric correlation is **.65**. Genetic effects on accomplishment are *larger* among those who practise.

### Cited Findings
- **Mosing et al. 2014.** All VERIFIED from full text: Mosing, M. A., Madison, G., Pedersen, N. L., Kuja-Halkola, R. & Ullén, F. (2014). Practice does not make perfect: No causal effect of music practice on music ability. *Psychological Science* 25(9), 1795–1803 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614541990); [PDF mirror](https://www.gwern.net/doc/genetics/heritable/correlation/2014-mosing.pdf)
  - **Sample.** 10,539 individuals in the STAGE cohort, including 2,569 complete pairs (1,211 MZ, 1,358 DZ). Data collected 2012–2013.
  - **How practice was measured.** Self-reported years played and hours/week in age bands 0–5, 6–11, 12–17 and 18+, summed to lifetime hours; non-players scored 0. Self-reported practice estimates are cited as having reliability .6–.9 (p. 1798).
  - **Who played and how much.** 80% of women and 62% of men had ever played. Among players, mean lifetime practice was **3,862 h for men (SE 72.46)** and **3,271 h for women (SE 52.86)**. These are standard errors, not SDs; the distribution is strongly right-skewed (p. 1799).
  - **Outcome.** The Swedish Musical Discrimination Test: pitch (27 trials), melody (18) and rhythm (18).
  - **Phenotypic correlations with raw hours** (Table 1):

    | Sex | Rhythm | Melody | Pitch |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Women | .18 | .29 | .28 |
    | Men | .25 | .36 | .35 |

    Log-hours values are similar. Note that the male pitch row in Table 1 prints internally inconsistent CIs.
  - **Heritability of practice hours** (Table 3): A = .69 in men; A = .41 and C = .21 in women. Ability heritability was 12–61%.
  - **MZ co-twin control.** Practice differences within identical pairs did not predict ability differences: melody r = .03, p = .43; pitch r = .05; rhythm r = .02. This held "despite the fact that some intrapair differences between twins were as great as 20,228 hr" (p. 1800).
- **Hambrick & Tucker-Drob, "The genetics of music accomplishment: Evidence for gene–environment correlation and interaction".** *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*; online 2014, usually cited as 2015. VERIFIED from PDF — [author PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/tucker-drob/files/2015/02/Hambrick-Tucker-Drob-2014-PBR-Genetics-of-Music-Accomplishment.pdf)
  - **Sample and measures.** National Merit Twin Study: 850 same-sex twin pairs (354 male, 496 female) who sat the National Merit Scholarship test in 1962 as high-school juniors (about age 17). Practice was categorical (not at all / occasionally / frequently). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0671-9)
  - **Accomplishment.** A dichotomous measure: for example a "Good" or better rating in a music contest, or a public performance. 269 of 1,677 respondents had an accomplishment, and 50 of those reported practising "not at all".
  - **Practice ↔ accomplishment.** Polychoric r = **.65**, "similar to the correlations between practice and objective measures of skilled performance observed in other studies of music".
  - **Variance components.**

    | Outcome | Genetic | Shared environment | Non-shared environment |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Accomplishment | 26% | 61% | 12% |
    | Practice | 38% | 44% | 19% |

  - **Mediation.** About one quarter of the genetic effect on accomplishment runs through practice.
  - **G × E.** Genetic effects on accomplishment were strongest among those who practise.
- **Mosing et al. (2024).** "Testing the Deliberate Practice Theory: Does Practice Reduce the Heritability of Musical Expertise?" SNIPPET-ONLY (search summary; the PMC page blocked automated fetch). — [PMC11433073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11433073/)
  - 6,471 twins.
  - Variation in musical expertise decreased with more practice hours, through lower shared-environment and residual variance.
  - The genetic component was not reduced by practice.

### Inferences
- **Which coefficient matches a playing-level quiz.** The relevant coefficients are the Pearson performance-outcome ones from Q2: r_c ≈ .52–.61, observed .44–.54, or corrected r² ≈ .27–.37. The .18–.36 from Mosing 2014 belongs to perception tests.
  - The polychoric .65 of Hambrick & Tucker-Drob is between coarse categorical self-reports.
  - It is not comparable to a Pearson r and should not be squared into a "variance explained".
- **What the twin data mean for hours-to-level numbers.** They say those numbers are **descriptive** ("people at level X typically report Y hours"), **not causal**. The same people who progress fast also choose to practise more, so the quiz should not imply that "doing Y hours will get you to X".
- **A general-population baseline.** Mosing's mean of about 3,300–3,900 lifetime hours among Swedes who ever played is a useful reference for "a typical person who once played an instrument". It is a mean of a skewed distribution, so the median is much lower (the median was not reported).

### Gaps
- No twin study uses an instrument-performance outcome measured by experts, and none looks specifically at guitarists.

## Q6. Starting-age effects and adult beginners on guitar

### Takeaway
Popular musicians start their main instrument about 2 years later than classical musicians (mean about 12.9 vs 10.5). They take lessons for about half as long (5.7 vs 10.5 years), and fewer take lessons at all (69% vs 90%). The conservatoire evidence on whether an early start pays off is mixed by instrument. **I found no reliable data on how fast adult beginners progress on guitar.**

### Cited Findings
- **VERIFIED.** Menze & Gembris 2018, Tab. 1 — [poster PDF](https://kw.uni-paderborn.de/fileadmin-kw/fakultaet/Institute/ibfm/Downloads/Poster_Played_it_till_my_fingers_bled_JM-HG_2018.pdf)

  | Measure | Classical, mean (SD) | Popular, mean (SD) | Test |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Age at starting any music | 8.65 (5.37), n = 92 | 10.87 (6.28), n = 326 | F = 9.52, p = .002 |
  | Age at starting main instrument | 10.54 (7.48) | 12.85 (7.35) | F = 7.02, p = .008 |
  | Years of lessons on main instrument | 10.48 (5.59), n = 84 | 5.67 (3.56), n = 222 | F = 79.25, p < .001, η² = .21 |

  - 90% of classical and 69% of popular musicians took lessons.
  - Self-rated level on the main instrument: classical 24% "very good" and 48% "good"; popular 15% "very good" and 55% "good". Average self-rated giftedness was 7.17/10 (SD 1.47), with no genre difference.
- **Jørgensen (2001), conservatoire students.** "Instrumental learning: is an early start a key to success?" *British Journal of Music Education* 18(3), 227–239. Abstract-level only (SNIPPET-ONLY): broken down by instrument, the relationship between an early start and high conservatoire performance is positive for some instruments and negative for others, with large individual differences. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0265051701000328)
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY.** Children who gave up music "had fallen behind in their accumulation of deliberate practice as early as age 8" (Sloboda et al. 1996, via Williamon & Valentine 2000) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712600161871)
- **VERIFIED (adult beginners).** The only adult-beginner guitar data found is Fender/YouGov: 72% of new US players are 13–34, 53% of beginners practise ≤2 h/week, and one in four report stagnating — [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/16-million-americans-learned-to-play-guitar-in-the-last-two-years-why-and-who-are-they-301394808.html)

### Inferences
- **What the late start implies.** Pop and rock guitarists start later, take fewer lessons and practise less per week. At a given age they will usually have far fewer formal-practice hours than classical peers, yet they rate their own level about the same. Self-assessment in the quiz may need genre-neutral, behaviour-anchored items rather than self-rated level.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal or diary study measuring adult-beginner guitar progress in hours to a milestone was found; no reliable source.
- Jørgensen's per-instrument results were not retrieved, and it is unknown whether guitar was in the sample.

### Inferences

  - The ratio is my inference.
  - The two figures also differ in instrument (classical instruments vs rock guitar) and in definition (estimated learning time vs retrospective practice).
- **Russian ДМШ guitar has no direct grade equivalent.** The 8-year curriculum totals 1,316 h for the main subject, of which 757 h is planned homework. Real homework practice is likely higher or lower than the norm. I found no source mapping ДМШ graduation to a UK grade.
